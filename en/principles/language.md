## Preamble

Smalltalk-2030 is an attempt to bring computers closer to human language.

If that is our aim, it would be strange for the project itself to turn around and tell people, “Speak English.”

English is a powerful tool for opening a project to the world.<br>
Japanese is a powerful tool for those who think in Japanese to think with depth, speed, precision, and freedom.

We refuse to give up either one.

We will not make an enemy of English.<br>
We will not make Japanese a second-class language.

Smalltalk-2030 seeks a place where Japanese and English coexist not in a hierarchy, but as two windows that widen the world for one another.

## Article 1 — The freedom to think in Japanese

Anyone participating in Smalltalk-2030 may think in Japanese, discuss in Japanese, and write about technology in Japanese.

Japanese must not be restricted merely because “technical work should be written in English.”

Thinking in one’s native language is not a failure of internationalism.<br>
It is a legitimate way to increase the resolution of thought.

## Article 2 — The responsibility to remain open to English

Smalltalk-2030 must not become a project enclosed within Japanese alone.

Information that matters beyond the Japanese-speaking community should, wherever practical, be reachable through English as well.

This does not mean that every text must always be completed in both languages at the same moment.

The absence of a translation is not itself a defect.<br>
A closed path to translation is.

## Article 3 — No hierarchy of originals

Neither the Japanese version nor the English version shall be inherently treated as the “real” text while the other is reduced to a translation.

Some texts may be born in Japanese.<br>
Others may be born in English.

What matters is not which language came first, but what thought is being carried by the text.

## Article 4 — Translation is not word replacement

The same meaning does not require the same grammar, sentence structure, or rhythm in both languages.

The Japanese version should read as natural Japanese.<br>
The English version should read as natural English.

Neither should become a shadow of the other.

The purpose of translation is not to preserve sentences. It is to **carry meaning across**.

## Article 5 — Do not encode hierarchy in URLs

As a rule, the website should treat Japanese and English as equal namespaces.

For example:

`/ja/`

and

`/en/`

The URL structure should not imply that one language is the main site and the other an appendix.

`smalltalk2030.jp` is the entrance to both.

## Article 6 — Do not choose a language on the user’s behalf

The site may use browser preferences or similar signals to suggest an appropriate language.

But the user’s own choice must take precedence.

The path between 日本語 and English should always be visible and understandable. A system must not hide that choice merely because it believes it knows better than the person using it.

## Article 7 — Do not confuse code with human language

Programming-language syntax, APIs, identifiers, and explanations written in natural language are different questions.

A technology does not need to be explained in English simply because it originated in the English-speaking world.

Likewise, explaining something in Japanese does not require rejecting established technical vocabulary used internationally.

Use what makes the idea clearest.

## Article 8 — Do not fear Japanese identifiers

Smalltalk-2030 does not confine Japanese to comments and documentation.

Where technical constraints and portability permit, the use of Japanese in identifiers should be considered directly and without embarrassment.

The custom that “program names must be English” is not a specification.

Customs may be questioned.

## Article 9 — Let Git history speak to both the world and the people doing the work

The essence of a change should be recorded in a form that developers around the world can follow.

At the same time, its background, motivation, design uncertainty, and cultural context do not need to be forced into English alone.

A concise shared language and a richer native-language explanation can coexist.

History is not a record for machines alone.<br>
It is also a letter to the humans who arrive later.

## Article 10 — Do not demand perfect symmetry

The two language editions do not need to have exactly the same number of pages, the same amount of text, or identical update times.

Bilingualism does not mean maintaining two mirror-image websites.

What matters is that a person entering through either language can reach the center of the project.

## Article 11 — An untranslated text still has value

A text may be published even if it exists in only one language.

We should not stop thought itself by imposing a rule that nothing may appear until its translation is complete.

When useful, untranslated status should be made clear.

And that incompleteness may itself become an invitation to translate.

## Article 12 — Welcome AI as a translator

AI may be used actively for translation, editing, comparison, and the creation of parallel texts.

But AI-generated language must not be treated as authority when no human involved understands what it says.

AI is a tool for lowering barriers between languages.

It is not an oracle for building new ones.

## Article 13 — Do not be ashamed when meanings diverge

When differences in expression or nuance appear between the Japanese and English versions, they should not automatically be dismissed as “translation errors.”

Thinking through an idea in two languages can expose ambiguity in the idea itself.

That is a bug, and it is also a discovery.

When necessary, revise both versions.

## Article 14 — Do not make English fluency a privileged class

English fluency is not a condition of participation.

The ability to write polished English and the ability to design something well are different skills.

A proposal must not be treated as less valuable merely because its author lacks confidence in English.

When translation is needed, humans or AI can help.

## Article 15 — Do not make non-Japanese speakers guests

By the same principle, people who do not understand Japanese must not be treated as outsiders visiting someone else’s project.

A participant who enters through English is still a participant in Smalltalk-2030.

Beginning in the Japanese-speaking world is not the same thing as belonging only to it.

## Article 16 — Put people before language policy

The purpose of this constitution is not to enforce correct language behavior.

If an exception makes it easier for someone to participate, make the exception.

If changing the form makes it easier for someone to think, change the form.

A language policy that makes human beings less free has failed its purpose.

## Article 17 — This constitution is itself translatable

This document shall exist in both Japanese and English.

The two versions do not need to be perfectly identical.

And if this constitution itself someday ceases to fit the thinking of Smalltalk-2030, it may be amended without hesitation.

Smalltalk-2030 carries the year 2030 in its name.

A project that names itself after the future must not allow rules written today to imprison that future.

## Closing principle

We will not give up English.

We will not give up Japanese.

Above all:

**Human beings should not have to adapt themselves to computers. Computers should move closer to human beings.**

We apply that principle not only to programming languages, but to the language of the project itself.

These are the language principles of Smalltalk-2030.
